FEA-optimized steel pipe

A 1 mm galvanized-steel pipe, cantilevered from a wall and full of water, redesigned over 49 simulation runs until it ranked first among all AUB teams.

Result
1st place
When
Spring 2026
Course
Mechanics of Materials, AUB
Tools
SolidWorks Simulation

Von Mises stress in the final design, peaking at 8.1 MPa. Post-processed from the SolidWorks Simulation results.

The problem

Carry water from an inlet fixed to a wall to a free outlet 2.0 m forward and 0.5 m to the side, through one 90° turn. Keep at least 15 cm² of flow area everywhere and at least 1 mm of wall. The pipe sags under its own weight and 37.5 N of water, and the lowest score wins:

Score = mass (kg) + 0.2 × outlet deflection (mm) + 80 × peak stress (GPa)

49 runs

We logged every run in a tracker and kept a change only if it lowered the score. Move along the chart to see what each version changed and what it did to the score.

Filled points lowered the best score and were kept. Hollow points were rejected. Use the arrow keys to step through versions.

What won

  • A circular section. Rectangular ducts stalled at 5.77 because the side arm twists the main run. A 44 mm tube with a 1 mm wall gives 15.20 cm² of flow with the least perimeter and the best torsional stiffness per gram.
  • A tapered web. A 1 mm plate on top of the 2 m arm, 200 mm tall at the wall and tapering to nothing at the elbow, follows the bending moment. Stiffness grows with the cube of the web's height but only linearly with its thickness.
  • A corner gusset. A 150 × 150 × 1 mm triangle inside the elbow relieves the stress concentration for about 0.09 kg.

Results

First designFinal designChange
Score10.175.18−49%
Outlet deflection10.76 mm1.11 mm−90%
Peak von Mises stress54.9 MPa8.10 MPa−85%
Mass3.63 kg4.31 kg+19%

The final study used 10,030 second-order tetrahedral elements and 19,888 nodes, with welded bodies under gravity and the water load. Spending 19% more mass on the web and gusset cut deflection and stress far more than it cost.

3-point bending test machine

A desktop machine that bends a specimen until it breaks and plots stress against strain while it does. Built for $130.

Result
Best Project Award, rank 1
Course and term
Engineering Materials, AUB, Fall 2025
Role
Team lead, team of 4
Tools
3D printing, Python

One test from start to fracture, taken from the recording.

Why build one

Commercial flexural testers cost thousands of dollars. We set out to build a compact machine that loads a beam at its center until it fails and records force and displacement throughout, at a small fraction of that cost.

How it works

  • Frame. A 3D-printed column and crosshead structure on an enclosed base, with two specimen supports and a wedge-shaped loading nose.
  • Drive. A stepper motor in the base turns a threaded rod through a flexible coupler and moves the crosshead.
  • Software. Custom Python software handles motor control and real-time force-displacement acquisition. It jogs the crosshead at a set speed, zeroes the reading, starts a test with one click, plots the data live and exports it as CSV. A specimen-shape setting lets it convert force to stress.

Result

The machine runs a full test to fracture and produces the curve below on its own software: stress climbs to about 100 MPa and the specimen breaks at about 2.9% strain.

It won the Best Project Award. The professor adopted the design as the model for future cohorts and proposed developing it further for installation in AUB labs.

CoreXY desktop 3D printer

The complete mechanical hardware of a desktop printer: frame, motion system, bed and electronics bay, designed for a stiffness and precision competition.

Status
Design frozen, competition in December 2026
When
Fall 2026
Team
4 people, Mechanical Design I, AUB
Tools
SolidWorks, ANSYS, Python

CAD model at the preliminary design stage. The frozen design replaces the X beam with a 20 × 20 × 2 mm tube.

Choosing the architecture

Four layouts were sized for the same 180 mm cube and 5,000 mm/s² acceleration, then scored on nine weighted criteria. CoreXY with a Z-moving bed scored 3.85 of 5, ahead of a fixed-gantry Cartesian at 3.65. It has the lightest moving mass, 0.73 kg, and keeps both motors on the frame.

To check that the result did not depend on our choice of weights, we ran 20,000 Monte Carlo trials with randomized weights. CoreXY stayed first in 94.3% of them, and in 67.6% when the scores were randomized as well.

The beam was twisting

The nozzle hangs 115 mm below the X beam, so a sideways push at the nozzle twists the beam as well as bending it. The preliminary calculation counted only bending and predicted 7.2 µm. FEA in ANSYS gave 52.1 µm, and a hand calculation with torsion included showed twist was 92% of it.

A 20 × 20 × 2 mm square tube has the same outer size as the original extrusion, so nothing else had to change. It cut the beam's deflection by 53% and it weighs less: 0.10 kg against 0.13 kg. Drag the handle to compare.

Extrusion, 52.1 µmTube, 24.6 µm

Total deformation under a 10 N load at the nozzle, ANSYS Workbench. Mesh refined to 86,000 nodes.

Shaft fatigue

The belt pulls each motor pulley in a fixed direction while its shaft turns, so every revolution fully reverses the bending stress. One shaft carried its pulley 40.5 mm out from the bearing, and a Shigley fatigue analysis gave it a safety factor of 1.74.

A 14 mm spacer under that motor shortens the overhang to 26.5 mm and raises the safety factor to 2.66. ANSYS agrees: 1.80 before, 2.78 after.

The design

  • Frame. 2020 aluminum extrusion, 19 pieces, with blind tapped joints. Designed travel is 206 × 182 × 185 mm at an estimated 8.7 kg.
  • Motion. CoreXY with 9 mm belts, linear rails on every axis.
  • Bed. A 200 × 200 × 6 mm cast aluminum plate on three lead screws, synchronized by one belt to a single hand crank that needs 0.86 N at the knob and holds position by itself.
  • Electronics bay. Room, ventilation and cable routing for a power supply and controller board.

Off-road micro-car

A four-wheel-drive contest car that runs on one motor. It finished second among AUB mechanical engineering teams.

Result
2nd place
When
Fall 2026
Role
Design and manufacturing lead, team of 4
Course
Manufacturing Processes I lab, AUB

The contest

Four events decide the ranking: a sprint, an obstacle course, a 30° ramp climb and a tug of war. Speed matters in the sprint and torque matters on the ramp and in the tug of war, so the gear ratio had to balance the two.

One motor, two belts

A single DC gear motor drives the first axle through a timing belt with a 3:1 reduction, which gives up some speed for the torque needed on the ramp and in the tug of war. A second belt under the chassis ties the two axles together, so all four wheels are driven and the car keeps pulling when one axle is in the air over a pipe.

We made the parts ourselves: lathe-turned steel shafts, a milled baseplate, bearing holders, motor and battery mounts, fused wiring and the belt drive. Each axle runs in ball bearings pressed into its holders under the baseplate.

Six-wheel RC dump truck

A six-wheel, skid-steer truck designed and manufactured to haul 2 kg, tip it onto a target, cross 15 cm gaps and climb a 45° ramp.

Status
Competition at the end of November 2026
When
Fall 2026
Course
Manufacturing Processes I, AUB
Tools
Fusion 360

Fusion 360 model, 305 components. In the tipping views the bed is posed at 38° about its hinge.

The brief

  • Fit within 450 × 250 mm. The model measures 445 × 244 mm and stands 193 mm tall.
  • Carry a 2 kg payload, tip it onto a floor target and bring the bed back.
  • Drive a T-shaped track and cross table gaps of 5, 10 and 15 cm.
  • Earn bonuses for climbing a 45° ramp and for the tightest turn, and score on payload per kilogram of truck.

Design

  • Six driven wheels. Identical drive modules, each a 12 V, 169 rpm gear motor on a 104 mm wheel. With three axles the truck always has two on the table while the third crosses a gap.
  • Skid steering. The truck turns in place like a tank to take the turn-radius bonus. The middle axle sits about 2 mm lower so the outer tires scrub less.
  • Geared tipping bed. A 35 kg·cm servo drives a 14-tooth pinion against a 42-tooth sector gear on the bed, tripling its torque. The bed pivots on a 6 mm pin in bronze bushings, and a limit switch reports when it is back down.
  • Electronics. An ESP32 controller with a radio receiver, two motor drivers, a fused 3S battery, an inertial sensor and four distance sensors, in a sealed cabinet.

Analysis

The model is parametric: one shared set of equations holds the measured bottle and motor dimensions and updates every part. Motor torque on the ramp, hinge torque for tipping at about 4 N·m, and the center of gravity were all sized by hand. The empty truck balances over its middle axle so it can reach across a gap.

Reconciling two competing blueprints before the build caught 10 engineering issues on paper, so the final truck was built with no major redesigns.
